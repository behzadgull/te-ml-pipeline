# Abstract

Machine-learning models that predict thermoelectric transport properties from composition are usually validated on rows drawn from the same chemical families they were trained on. Materials discovery asks a harder question: how well does a model predict a family it has never seen? We answer it on a pinned snapshot of the Starrydata2 database, in which every composition is assigned by frozen, pre-registered rules to one of <!--v:n_named-->27<!--/v--> chemically defined families, to an "other oxide" category, or to an unassignable category (<!--v:un_row_share-->23.3%<!--/v--> of rows). A family is held out for a property when it has at least <!--v:min_clusters-->30<!--/v--> chemistry clusters and <!--v:min_rows-->1,000<!--/v--> rows, which leaves <!--v:q_S-->22<!--/v-->, <!--v:q_sigma-->23<!--/v-->, <!--v:q_kappa-->17<!--/v--> and <!--v:q_zT-->19<!--/v--> held-out families for the Seebeck coefficient, electrical conductivity, thermal conductivity and zT. For each, we will compare a model trained with the family absent against the same model with the family present, and against two controls that remove the same number of training rows at random or remove a different whole family. We will then decompose the held-out error into a family-level offset, a scale error and lost within-family signal, and test whether a model trained on the family alone outperforms the pooled model. This preview reports the design, the family labels and the held-out units. Production runs are pending, and no model result is reported.

# 1. Introduction

Composition-based models of the Seebeck coefficient, electrical conductivity, thermal conductivity and figure of merit are now trained on tens of thousands of literature measurements [@katsura2025starrydata; @na2022public]. Their reported accuracy depends heavily on how the data are split. Random splits place near-duplicate measurements of the same material on both sides of the split and overstate accuracy; grouping by composition or by chemistry cluster removes much of that inflation [@meredig2018machine; @li2024mdhit; @jia2024dealing; @companionA]. Grouped cross-validation still tests a model on chemistries that sit inside families it was trained on. A clustered split holds out PbTe doped with Na while PbSe, SnTe and PbTe doped with other elements remain in training.

Discovery rarely works that way. A model is most useful when it is asked about a class of compounds that is underexplored or absent from the training data, and that is exactly where composition-based models have the least support. Evidence on this point for thermoelectrics is sparse. The study that introduced the ESTM dataset found that zT prediction for material groups absent from training reached an R² of only 0.13 before transfer learning [@na2022public]. On a 3,879-row subset of that dataset, Ho et al. compared random, composition-wise and family-wise single splits and found negative R² for every target under the family-wise split; a constant predictor equal to the training mean was also negative in every seed and target [@ho2026physicsinspired]. Both results point to a large failure, but neither separates its causes. A negative R² on a held-out family can arise because the family's typical property values differ from everything in training, because the model cannot rank compositions within the family, or simply because the training set became smaller.

These causes have different consequences. If held-out error is mostly a family-level offset, the model's ranking of candidates within the family may still be useful, and a few measurements from the new family could recalibrate it. If the model has lost the within-family signal, no recalibration helps. And if the loss is no larger than that from removing the same number of rows at random, the problem is data volume, not chemistry.

This paper addresses three questions, all fixed before any model was trained (Section 2.10).

(a) How much accuracy does a pooled composition model lose on a family it has never seen, and is that loss larger than the loss from removing an equal amount of other data, either scattered at random or as a different whole family?

(b) When the pooled model fails on an unseen family, how much of its error is a family-level offset, how much a scale error, and how much lost within-family signal?

(c) For families with enough data, does a model trained on the family alone outperform the pooled model on that family?

We answer them for four properties and two model classes of different capacity, at family level and, as a sensitivity analysis, for chemically related families held out together. The companion paper [@companionA] established on the same data how validation choices inflate reported accuracy and where the remaining headroom lies. This paper asks what happens beyond the edge of the families a model has seen.

# 2. Methods

## 2.1 Data

We use the same pinned Starrydata2 snapshot [@katsura2025starrydata], cleaning pipeline and featurisation as the companion paper [@companionA]: 397 features per row (132 MAGPIE attributes [@ward2016general] computed with matminer [@ward2018matminer], 264 CBFV features [@murdock2020domain] on the Oliynyk element-property set [@oliynyk2016highthroughput], and temperature). The dataset is identified by its SHA-256 hash, and every run verifies the hash before it starts. <!-- src: CLAUDE.md canonical dataset; paper_b/SHARED_DEPENDENCIES.md --> The snapshot has <!--v:n_rows-->280,173<!--/v--> rows in <!--v:n_hosts-->8,908<!--/v--> chemistry clusters; the number of rows with a value differs by property (Table 1). Electrical and thermal conductivity are modelled and scored in log10 space, the Seebeck coefficient and zT in linear space, as in the companion paper.

## 2.2 Chemistry clusters

Rows are grouped by the chemistry-cluster identifier of the companion paper: elements present below 5 atomic percent are treated as dopants and removed, the remaining amounts are snapped to the nearest integer when within 5% of it, and the formula is reduced [@yeh2004nanostructured; @ma2025reexamining]. Every cross-validation split in this paper keeps chemistry clusters intact, so that no host composition appears on both sides of any split.

## 2.3 Family labels

Each chemistry cluster's host formula is assigned to a material family by an ordered list of rules; the first rule that matches wins. A rule specifies the allowed elements on each crystallographic site and the stoichiometric ratio between sites, with a uniform tolerance of 15% on site ratios (20% for the liquid-like Cu₂X/Ag₂X family). The tolerance accounts for a known artefact: removing a substituted dopant makes its site appear deficient. Where two structural classes share a stoichiometry, the majority cation decides (for example SnSe-type versus rock-salt IV–VI compounds). Oxides are split into cobaltites, manganites, perovskite titanates, ZnO-based and In₂O₃-based families by majority transition metal or cation, with an "other oxide" category for the rest. Table 1 lists the <!--v:n_named-->27<!--/v--> named families, the other-oxide category and the unassignable category. <!-- src: paper_b/config/families.yaml (v2.2) -->

The rules were drafted before any family counts were computed and revised three times (v1 to <!--v:labels_version-->v2.2<!--/v-->) after reviewing unassignable hosts and a random audit sample of up to <!--v:audit_n-->15<!--/v--> hosts per family (<!--v:audit_n_unassignable-->30<!--/v--> for the unassignable category; all hosts where a family has fewer), always before any model was trained. Every revision, its reasons and its full output are retained. <!-- src: paper_b/reports/family_labels/ (v1, v2, v2.1, v2.2, final) --> The final labels were produced from a clean commit. ## 2.4 Held-out units

A family is held out for a property only if it has at least <!--v:min_clusters-->30<!--/v--> chemistry clusters and at least <!--v:min_rows-->1,000<!--/v--> rows for that property. <!--v:min_clusters-->30<!--/v--> clusters give at least <!--v:clusters_per_fold_word-->six<!--/v--> clusters per fold in <!--v:n_folds_word-->five<!--/v-->-fold grouped cross-validation, and <!--v:min_rows-->1,000<!--/v--> rows give roughly <!--v:min_rows_per_fold-->200<!--/v--> test rows per fold for a stable error decomposition. The threshold was committed before any family counts existed. <!-- src: paper_b/config/paper_b.yaml; commit 6f88f9d --> The unassignable and "other oxide" categories are never held out, because neither is a family; both always remain in the training pool. ## 2.5 Experimental conditions

For each property *t* and held-out family *F*, the rows of *F* are split into <!--v:n_folds_word-->five<!--/v--> chemistry-cluster folds, repeated <!--v:repeats_word-->three<!--/v--> times with different seeds. All conditions use these identical folds, which are generated once and committed before any run. <!-- src: paper_b/results/splits/20260929T113055/ --> Each test fold of *F* is predicted under four conditions that differ only in the training data (Table 2).

- **C0, pooled.** Training on all rows except the test fold. This is ordinary within-distribution performance on *F*, with the rest of *F* available in training.
- **C1, leave-one-family-out.** Training on all rows except every row of *F*. The model has never seen the family.
- **C2, size-matched random removal.** As C0, minus a random sample of rows from outside *F*, drawn so that the training set has exactly the size of C1's. This tests whether C1's loss is explained by having fewer training rows.
- **C3, structured removal.** As C0, minus one whole other family *G*: the held-out family closest to *F* in row count for property *t*, chosen outside *F*'s super-family (Section 2.8), never the unassignable or "other oxide" categories, with ties broken alphabetically. This tests whether losing any coherent region of chemistry costs as much as losing *F*'s own relatives.

If C1 falls well below C2 and C3, which remain close to C0, the loss is specific to the absence of *F*'s relatives.

## 2.6 Family specialists

A specialist model is trained on *F*'s training folds only and evaluated on the same test folds as C0, so the comparison of specialist and pooled model is paired fold by fold.

## 2.7 Models and hyperparameters

Two model classes of different capacity are used and reported separately, never averaged: gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost [@chen2016xgboost]) and ridge regression on standardised features [@pedregosa2011scikit].

Hyperparameters for the pooled conditions are tuned once per property, family and model class, on the rows excluding *F*, by an Optuna search of 20 trials scored with inner three-fold chemistry-cluster cross-validation, over the same search space as the companion paper. The tuned values are shared by C0 to C3. Tuning never sees *F*, so C1 is free of hyperparameter leakage, and the four conditions differ only in their training data.

Specialists are tuned separately, nested inside each outer training fold, with 10 trials. The smaller budget can only understate a specialist's performance; a positive specialist-minus-pooled difference is therefore conservative, and a null difference is reported with this caveat.

## 2.8 Super-family sensitivity analysis

Holding out one family leaves its chemical relatives in training: when GeTe-type compounds are held out, rock-salt IV–VI and GeTe–Sb₂Te₃ pseudobinary compounds remain. To test how much this softens the family-level results, three super-families defined before any run are held out as single units: IV–VI (rock salt, GeTe-type, SnSe-type and the pseudobinaries), oxides (cobaltites, manganites, perovskite titanates, ZnO-based, In₂O₃-based) and Zintl (1-2-2, Mg₃X₂-type and 14-1-11). A super-family qualifies for a property under the same threshold applied to the union of its members. All <!--v:n_standalone_families-->15<!--/v--> other families are rerun as standalone units for C3 only, so that the structured-removal control is defined consistently. <!-- src: paper_b/config/super_families.yaml -->

## 2.9 Metrics

All metrics are computed per property, family, condition and model class, in each property's scoring space, and are never pooled across held-out families.

**Coefficient of determination.** R² relative to the held-out family's own mean, reported as the mean and standard deviation over the <!--v:repeats_word-->three<!--/v--> repeats. The standard deviation describes split-to-split variability and is not a confidence interval.

**Skill relative to the training mean.** skill_train = 1 − MSE_model / MSE_c, where *c* is the mean of the target over the training rows of that condition. This is what a predictor without any information about *F* could achieve, and it is the baseline used by Ho et al. [@ho2026physicsinspired].

**Error decomposition.** The mean squared error on *F* is split exactly into three terms (Figure 4):

$$\mathrm{MSE} = \underbrace{(\bar{\hat y} - \bar y)^2}_{\text{family offset}} + \underbrace{(s_{\hat y} - r\,s_y)^2}_{\text{scale error}} + \underbrace{(1 - r^2)\,s_y^2}_{\text{lost within-family signal}},$$

where $\bar y$ and $s_y$ are the mean and (population) standard deviation of the true values on *F*, $\bar{\hat y}$ and $s_{\hat y}$ those of the predictions, and *r* their correlation. We report each term's share of the MSE together with *r* itself.

![Figure 4](paper_b/figures/fig4_error_decomposition.png){width=100%}

*Figure 4. The three terms of the error decomposition, illustrated with synthetic data; these are not model results. (a) Predictions follow the true ranking within the family but sit above it: the error is almost entirely a family offset, the within-family correlation r is high, and a few measurements from the new family would recalibrate the model. (b) Predictions barely follow the true ranking: most of the error is lost within-family signal, r is low, and no recalibration helps. The bars show each term's share of the mean squared error; the shares sum to one.*

**Uncertainty.** 95% percentile intervals come from a cluster bootstrap over *F*'s chemistry clusters (1,000 seeded resamples). Differences between conditions (C0 − C1, C0 − C2, C0 − C3, specialist − C0) use paired resamples, the same clusters drawn for both conditions.

## 2.10 Pre-registration, implementation and reproducibility

The objectives, family rules, threshold, conditions, metrics and number of repeats were committed to the repository before any model was trained, and every later change is recorded as a dated amendment with its reason. <!-- src: paper_b/docs/Paper_B_Objectives_Methodology_v3_FROZEN.md --> The harness enforces the design with assertions that abort a run if the held-out family appears in any tuning or C1 training set, if a test fold appears in C0, C2 or C3 training, if C2's training size differs from C1's, if *G* appears in C3 training, or if a specialist sees rows outside *F*'s training folds. Every run records the dataset hash, the family-label and split-manifest hashes, the code commit and whether the working tree was clean. A <!--v:n_checks_word-->17<!--/v-->-check test suite covering these assertions, checkpoint and resume, crash recovery, determinism and the metric calculations has passed on Windows and, at commit <!--v:linux_commit-->6b57be0<!--/v-->, on Linux; it is to be repeated on the final commit before any production run. <!-- src: paper_b/results/smoke_test_linux/ -->

Pooled conditions will run on GPU hardware to be specified and specialists on CPU. Because CPU and GPU implementations of gradient boosting need not produce identical models, specialists for three zT families chosen in advance (the largest, median and smallest qualifying) will also be run on GPU, and any specialist comparison in which the CPU-GPU difference exceeds 10% of the specialist-minus-pooled difference will be flagged.

# 3. Results

## 3.1 Families

<!-- BEGIN SECTION 3.1 -->
The labelled snapshot has 280,173 rows in 8,908 chemistry clusters. The 27 family rules assign 4,116 clusters (46.2%) and 191,510 rows (68.4%) to a named family, and the other-oxide rule a further 1,210 clusters (13.6%) and 23,481 rows (8.4%). The remaining 3,582 clusters (40.2%) and 65,182 rows (23.3%) match no rule and are labelled unassignable (Table 1, Figure 2b). For each unassignable host, the nearest rule fails because an element lies outside it for 48.0% of hosts, because a site ratio is outside tolerance for 31.3% and because a required site is empty for 14.4%; the rest fail on other conditions of that rule. Unassignable hosts stay in every training pool but are never a held-out family.

Family sizes are very unequal (Figure 2a). IV–VI rock salt is the largest, with 475 clusters and 30,202 rows; the three largest families (IV–VI rock salt, Skutterudite and Half-Heusler) hold 40.0% of the rows assigned to a named family. At the other end, MgAgSb has 1 cluster and 389 rows.

A family qualifies for a property if it has at least 30 chemistry clusters and 1,000 rows with a value for that property (Section 2.4). Of the 27 named families, 22 qualify for S, 23 qualify for σ, 17 qualify for κ and 19 qualify for zT, giving 81 family-property units (Figure 3). Of these families, 17 qualify for all four properties and 4 for none (Zintl 14-1-11, Tetrahedrite, MgAgSb and In₂O₃-based). Of the 27 family-property pairs that fall below the threshold, 11 fail on rows only, 2 on clusters only and 14 on both. Together, the qualifying families hold 66.1% of the S rows, 69.7% of the σ rows, 67.1% of the κ rows and 69.2% of the zT rows, so held-out results describe these slices of each property's data, not all of it.

The three super-families (Section 2.8) qualify for all four properties as unions, giving 12 super-family units with between 3,448 and 28,363 rows each. Several members that fall below the threshold alone are carried by these unions: GeTe–Sb₂Te₃ pseudobinary for S, κ and zT, In₂O₃-based for all four properties, ZnO-based for κ and zT and Zintl 14-1-11 for all four properties. The other 15 families belong to no super-family and are standalone units, with 13 qualifying for S, 13 qualifying for σ, 9 qualifying for κ and 11 qualifying for zT (46 units in all); they are rerun for the structured-removal control only.

## 4.1 Limitations

Families are assigned from composition alone, not from crystal structure, and <!--v:un_row_share-->23.3%<!--/v--> of rows match no family; held-out results describe the families that could be labelled, not the whole snapshot. The "other oxide" category and unassignable rows remain in every training set, so held-out families are never entirely without distant chemical relatives. Family size varies widely, so the controls are matched within each comparison but results across families rest on different amounts of data. Specialist tuning uses half the pooled search budget, which can only understate specialists. Results apply to composition-based descriptors and to the two model classes tested. As in the companion paper, labels carry measurement and digitisation noise that bounds every condition equally.
